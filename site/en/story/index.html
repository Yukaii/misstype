Misstype: The Story & Technical Report

Author: Yukai Huang · Updated: October 2026 · Stack: Zig / WebAssembly / C ABI

How an urge to type in the dark on an overnight bus led from split touchpad prototypes and language model inspirations to rewriting an input method engine in Zig and WebAssembly.

1. The Spark: Midnight Bus & Typing in the Dark

The origin of Misstype traces back to an overnight long-distance highway bus ride.

In the quiet hours past midnight, the cabin lights were extinguished, and passengers around me were fast asleep. Through the window, faint outlines of highway lights passed by against an endless dark sky. In moments like that, creative ideas, urgent notes, and conversations rush through your head.

Yet I couldn’t—and didn’t want to—turn on the glaring overhead reading light. It would disturb sleeping neighbors and ruin the calm darkness. But trying to type on a laptop keyboard or phone screen in pitch darkness was an exercise in pure frustration: without seeing the physical key labels, any minor finger displacement resulted in jumbled, unrecognizable characters. On a glass touchscreen, slipping by a few millimeters meant constantly mashing Backspace to delete whole sentences.

“What if you could type continuously in pitch darkness without turning on a single light, relying purely on muscle memory and forgiving software?”

That sparked an idea: the ideal input surface wasn’t a rigid mechanical keyboard, but two split miniature touchpads. One in each hand, or resting comfortably on your lap. Your fingers wouldn’t have to hunt for exact mechanical keycaps; you could simply tap in approximate regions from muscle memory.

2. Prototyping: Split Surfaces & Spatial Uncertainty

Inspired by that thought, I began building early software and hardware prototypes (milestones M0 through M2 in our project notes).

However, touch typing without tactile key boundaries surfaced a fundamental mathematical reality: boundary-free input is inherently noisy and probabilistic.

On a mechanical switch, a key is either pressed or it isn't. On a flat touchpad, each tap is a continuous coordinate point (x, y) with associated timing:

  • A tap might land a couple millimeters off-center into the territory of a neighboring key (adjacent spatial slips).
  • When typing rapidly with two hands, the order of events between the left and right hand frequently swaps (transposition).
  • Quick finger glides might under-press or omit a medial phonetic mark entirely.

Traditional deterministic keystroke matching fell apart completely. What was required was a probabilistic decoding pipeline: accepting a continuous stream of noisy, timestamped phonetic tokens with uncertainty and finding the most likely intended sentence through dynamic lattice search.

3. The Catalyst: The Jev Model & LM-Assisted Disambiguation

Around the time I was grappling with how to rank phonetic lattice paths, lightweight generative and scoring models emerged—notably the announcement of the Jev model by Typesafe AI.

Jev focused on lightning-fast token scoring and constrained candidate ranking. The realization was immediate: Candidate disambiguation and auto-selection in an input method is an ideal domain for probabilistic language scoring.

Shortly thereafter, an engineer in Japan released an experimental IME that used on-the-fly model prediction to seamlessly detect Japanese versus English keystrokes in a single stream—eliminating the need to ever press a language-switching key.

These developments proved that the 30-year-old input method paradigm—typing exact syllable codes, pausing to open a popup menu, and pressing arrow keys to select words—was fundamentally outdated.

4. The Pivot: Fixing 30 Years of Zhuyin Frustration

While working on the split touchpad prototype, an obvious realization hit me:

“Why am I spending all my time fabricating custom hardware when the software Zhuyin IME I use on my computer every single day is so frustrating to use?”

For Traditional Chinese users typing with standard Zhuyin (Bopomofo), the daily typing experience has long suffered from persistent friction:

  1. Mandatory tone keys: Consonants and vowels sit in the main typing rows, but tone marks (ˊ ˇ ˋ ˙) are relegated to the number row or outer punctuation keys. For every single syllable, fingers must reach up awkwardly. Omit a tone mark, and the IME gets confused or drops the syllable.
  2. Zero tolerance for mistakes: Type slightly too quickly and swap two letters (e.g., typing urp instead of rup for ㄐㄧㄣ), graze an adjacent key, or drop a medial vowel, and traditional IMEs immediately output red error squiggles or gibberish. You are forced to stop, backspace, and retype.
  3. Fractured mental flow: Natural writing thrives on momentum. Having to stop every two or three characters to glance at a candidate menu, press number keys, and hit Enter destroys cognitive focus.

The goal pivoted sharply: Why not build a modern, forgiving Zhuyin input method first?

The design goals were crystal clear: no tones required, swapped keys restored, adjacent slips corrected, dropped symbols recovered, and full-sentence decoding on the fly.

“Just make another, and it starts well.” The first prototype demonstrated that fuzzy lattice decoding with toneless Bopomofo wasn’t just workable—it felt astonishingly liberating.

5. The Technical Leap: Rewriting the Core in Zig

The initial concept was prototyped in Python and Swift (MisstypeCore) for macOS.

While Swift felt comfortable on macOS, taking Misstype to Linux (fcitx5 and IBus) and ensuring long-term multi-platform portability exposed serious hurdles:

  • Bulky runtime dependencies: Running Swift on Linux required shipping heavy Swift runtime shared objects. CI Docker images ballooned to nearly 4 GB, and cross-compilation was cumbersome.
  • Memory allocation overhead: An input method must react within microseconds on every keystroke. Constructing candidate lattices, running beam searches, and computing bigram transitions incurred measurable overhead under Swift's ARC and frequent heap allocations.

To achieve true zero-dependency distribution, deterministic memory management, and sub-millisecond latency, I made the decision to rewrite the entire decoding engine from scratch in Zig (core-zig/).

Why Zig?

Aspect Swift Prototype Era Zig Core (Current)
C ABI Export Requires @_cdecl wrappers and glue code Native first-class C ABI exporting misstype.h
Cross-Compilation Heavy platform-specific build images Single pinned compiler binary; cross-compiles to x86_64, aarch64, and wasm32
Memory Architecture ARC reference counting, scattered heap allocations Arena Allocator: Keystroke decode runs in an arena, freed in one O(1) step
Decode Latency ~1ms to 25ms (process and object churn) 0.1ms to 0.5ms (pure local compute, completely imperceptible)

The Zig rewrite created an exceptionally clean architecture. The macOS Input Method Kit adapter and the Linux fcitx5 / IBus plugins link against the exact same shared library, guaranteeing 100% identical decoding behavior with zero external runtime bloat.

6. Breaking Boundaries: WebAssembly & The Offline Markdown Editor

With a pure Zig core free of system dependencies, compiling directly to WebAssembly (wasm32-wasi) became effortless.

This directly bypassed a long-standing operating system barrier:

On iPadOS, Apple strictly forbids third-party input methods when using external hardware keyboards. Users have been locked into the default system keyboard for years.

Since Misstype's decoder runs natively in WebAssembly in the browser, why not build a focused Markdown editor that hosts the IME directly?

That became the Misstype Markdown Editor (PWA):

  • In-editor keyboard capture: Built on ProseMirror, the editor captures physical hardware key events via JavaScript and passes them directly to misstype.wasm in real time.
  • Complete offline capability: Precached through Service Workers, the editor requires zero internet connectivity after its initial load, working reliably on flights or offline trips.
  • Use anywhere with zero installation: On a library terminal, a locked-down company workstation, a school computer, or a friend’s laptop, you can open the browser and write with Misstype immediately—no installer or admin privileges required.

7. Conclusion: Built for Our Own Hands

Looking back from that late-night bus ride to the current ecosystem spanning macOS, Linux, and WebAssembly, Misstype grew from a personal itch into a full-featured, open-source tool.

It was never conceived around corporate roadmaps or artificial growth metrics. It exists because typing in Chinese shouldn't feel like navigating a minefield of tone keys, rigid sequences, and candidate popups. Even if a finger slips or two keys swap, getting the sentence you meant without missing a beat brings a feeling of genuine typing freedom.

If you are tired of having your writing rhythm interrupted by your input method, give Misstype a try—either by installing the desktop package or opening the online editor to type your first sentence.
